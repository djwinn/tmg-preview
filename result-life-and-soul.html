You tend to show up as

The Life & Soul

“You're so much fun.”

It's what people say about you, and it's true. But while it might look that way from the outside, there's often more going on underneath.

This is a starting point, not a label. Most of us recognise ourselves in more than one of these patterns, because we learned different ways to stay safe at different times.

Always on

You're good with people. You're the one who gets the table laughing, who remembers everyone's name, who says yes to the party and makes it better by turning up. People like having you around, and they mean it.

You're always on. If there's something happening, you'd rather be there than hear about it afterwards.

And then you drive home.

Something settles. It isn't sadness, exactly. It's the particular quiet of having spent an evening with people who enjoyed your company, and feeling that none of them really saw you.

In my twenties, being the life and soul mattered to me more than almost anything. I hadn't had that as a teenager. I think a lot of us who came out a little later know that feeling of catching up.

Being wanted felt like being loved

Maybe somewhere along the way, being wanted started to feel like being loved. Being fun, interesting, good to be around: perhaps that's what helped you feel accepted and connected.

You built a life full of people, and there's nothing wrong with that. Your warmth is real, and people feel it when you walk in.

Liked, but not known

It can be exhausting. Especially if part of you is quieter than the man people see at the party.

And you can be very good at being liked, and very bad at being known. Lots of friends, lots of plans, and very few people you'd call at two in the morning.

I've asked myself this more than once. What is the risk of me saying I'm not happy? When everyone expects you to be the fun one, saying it can feel like letting the whole room down.

A different kind of belonging

Nobody is asking you to stop being the man who lights up a room.

But you might begin to see that being liked by lots of people isn't the same as feeling close to someone. You don't always have to be funny, interesting or entertaining. You can have a quiet evening. You can admit you're struggling. And if someone loses interest, maybe they weren't the people you most needed anyway.

You might discover what it feels like to be wanted for who you are, not just for what you bring to the party.

What would it feel like to walk into a room, not have to impress anyone, not have to be the fun one, and simply be yourself?

Need more support?

Gay Men Connect is a space for connection and conversation, not a substitute for professional mental health support. If you're struggling, please speak to a qualified mental health professional. If you're in immediate danger, contact your local emergency services.

  • Samaritans: call free on 116 123, any time, day or night
  • Switchboard LGBT+: call free on 0800 0119 100
  • In the US: call or text 988
  • Pink Therapy: a directory of therapists who work with gender and sexual diversity, at pinktherapy.com
  • If you're in danger right now, call 999, or your local emergency number.
